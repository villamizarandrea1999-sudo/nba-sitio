Mark Williams Undergoes Shoulder Surgery, Suns Left Scrambling at Center

Phoenix's projected starting five just lost a shoulder for months, not weeks. Here's what the labrum tear means for a Suns team already short on margin for error out West.

By NBA GRID Staff • September 12, 2026 • 4 min read

Mark Williams went under the knife this week to repair a torn labrum in his left shoulder, and the timing could not sting more for the Phoenix Suns. Williams was penciled in as the team's starting center for 2026-27, the anchor of a frontcourt that was supposed to solve Phoenix's rebounding problems from a year ago. Instead, camp opens with him on the shelf.

Phoenix now turns to Oso Ighodaro and rookie Khaman Maluach to cover minutes at center.

KEY TAKEAWAY

The Suns haven't put a hard number on the recovery, but people close to the situation are pointing to roughly five months — meaning Williams likely doesn't see the floor again until somewhere around the All-Star break.

A Shoulder Injury Nobody Saw Coming

There was no single, dramatic collision that set this off — no viral clip of a fall everyone will point back to later. Labrum tears in big men often build up quietly, the product of years of banging for post position and fighting for rebounds, until one motion finally does enough damage that surgery becomes the only real option. That appears to be roughly what happened here, and it's part of why the recovery window is measured in months rather than weeks: shoulder labrum repairs, especially for a 7-footer who makes his living with his upper body, tend to take longer to fully trust again than knee or ankle procedures do.

Who Actually Plays Center Now

The job falls to Oso Ighodaro first, with rookie Khaman Maluach — the Suns' first-round pick out of Duke — thrown into the deep end far sooner than Phoenix's coaching staff would have preferred. Ighodaro brings the kind of switchable, high-motor defense that keeps a lineup afloat, but he's never been asked to anchor a starting frontcourt for a full season. Maluach has the physical tools everyone raves about, yet nothing replaces game reps, and now he's getting them out of necessity instead of on a development curve the franchise controlled.

What It Costs Phoenix in the West

Losing a starting-caliber center for the first four or five months doesn't just hurt on paper — it changes how the Suns can play. Rim protection thins out, second-chance points get harder to prevent, and the margin for error in a crowded Western Conference gets even tighter. Phoenix wasn't projected as a top-four seed even with Williams healthy; without him for most of the first half of the season, the front office may have to get creative before the trade deadline just to stay in the play-in picture.
